# HistoryRPG — Level 4 Script: The Road to Revolution

Oct 23, 2026 · @Travis

## Level 4 at a glance

This is the first full draft of the prototype level's script, built from your lessons, DBQ, test, and the bible. To fit one 50-minute period, the main quest keeps seven beats at about 40 minutes; everything else moved to side content for home play, and the Chronicle Trial can be its own day or homework.

**Level question:** How did tighter British control lead to armed conflict?

**Script conventions:** speaker names in caps; player choices are numbered under "Sivart chooses"; \[brackets\] are stage directions for the build; Archive Page IDs (AP-01, AP-02...) link to the source list near the end.

## Voice guide

Dialogue stays short (one to three sentences per box), plain-language, and readable for 8th graders, with hard words linked to the glossary.

| Character | Voice | Sounds like | Never |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sivart | Curious, a little sarcastic, modern | "Wait, they're taxing *paper*? Like, all of it?" | Changes history or acts as the hero who fixes it |
| Graham Senoj (Mr. Senoj) | Calm, dry humor, asks questions instead of giving answers | "Interesting. And who told you that? Why would they want you to believe it?" | Lectures for more than two boxes |
| Kit | Boston printer's apprentice, 14, quick, proud of his city, starts Patriot-leaning but listens | "Every pamphlet in this town comes through our shop. That makes us the most dangerous boys in Boston." | Sounds modern |
| Ret Siger | Over-the-top teacher energy, puns, always mid-lesson | "Pencils down, Sons of Liberty! Today's quiz is worth five candies." | Jokes in serious scenes |
| Historical figures | Their own words where possible, simplified when needed | Pulled from Archive Pages | Invented quotes presented as real |

## Script: opening and Beats 1–3

### Opening — The Archive

&#91;The Living Record lies open on Graham Senoj's desk. The page titled "1754–1775" is gray and blank.\]

**MR. SENOJ:** Twenty-one years. In 1754 most colonists were proud to be British. By 1775 some of them were shooting at British soldiers. That page is supposed to explain how.

**SIVART:** And it's blank because… the Fade?

**MR. SENOJ:** Because someone decided one side of the story was enough. Bring me evidence from both sides, and the page writes itself back in. Here's your question.

&#91;Level question appears in the Record: "How did tighter British control lead to armed conflict?"\]

**MR. SENOJ:** Start where it starts: a war in the woods, and a very young officer who's about to lose.

### Beat 1 — The Scout (Ohio frontier, 1754)

&#91;Forest map. Sivart arrives near a small, round log stockade in a muddy meadow. Virginia militia dig trenches in the rain.\]

**KIT:** You're the new scout? Good. I'm only here because my uncle's in the militia and somebody had to carry the paper and ink. Colonel Washington wants eyes on the French.

**GEORGE WASHINGTON:** \[22 years old, soaked, serious\] Scout. The French claim this whole valley and have built forts to hold it. Virginia claims it too. Find out how many are coming.

&#91;Scouting minigame: sneak through the woods, count French and allied Native soldiers, avoid being seen. Lite challenge: no combat.\]

**SIVART:** There are a lot of them. Way more than us.

**WASHINGTON:** Then we hold this fort as long as we can.

&#91;Cutscene card: July 3, 1754. After a day of fighting in the rain, Washington surrenders Fort Necessity to the French. It is the opening of the French and Indian War.\]

**KIT:** We lost. But Britain will send its army now. And armies cost money… somebody's going to have to pay for all this.

&#91;Time-skip card: 1763. Britain wins the war. France gives up Canada and its lands east of the Mississippi. Britain's national debt has nearly doubled.\]

**Record check — Sivart chooses:** Why does the end of this war matter for the colonies?

1. Britain won a lot of land and now needs money to pay for the war and protect it. ✔
2. The colonists won independence from France and Britain.
3. France now controls the Ohio Valley.

&#91;Correct choice unlocks Historidex: George Washington. Wrong choices get a hint from Mr. Senoj: "Look at the map. Whose color covers it now?"\]

### Beat 2 — The Line (frontier trading post, 1763)

&#91;A British officer nails a proclamation to a post. Settlers with wagons crowd around. A Lenape trader watches from the side.\]

**BRITISH OFFICER:** By order of His Majesty: no settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains. All lands beyond are reserved for the Indian nations.

**SETTLER:** We fought that war for this land! Now we can't touch it?

**LENAPE TRADER:** You fought the French. Nobody asked us. Pontiac's warriors have already burned British forts this summer because settlers keep coming.

&#91;Perspective Lens unlocks: the player views the line from the settler's side, the trader's side, and the officer's side. Each view adds a sentence to the Record.\]

**OFFICER:** \[Lens view\] Ten thousand soldiers to keep peace on this frontier. Do you know what that costs?

**Record check — Sivart chooses:** Why did Britain create the Proclamation Line?

1. To punish the colonists for losing Fort Necessity.
2. To avoid more costly wars with Native nations on the frontier. ✔
3. To give the land to France.

&#91;Archive Page unlocked: AP-07, Proclamation Line map. Side quest marker appears: Pontiac's War.\]

### Beat 3 — Pay Up (Boston, 1764–1767)

&#91;Boston map opens: Kit's print shop, the docks, the Custom House, a tavern. Kit is now a printer's apprentice in the city.\]

**KIT:** Welcome to Boston. Fair warning, every week there's a new law from London and every week we print the angry pamphlet about it.

&#91;Act chain: Sivart visits four stations around town in any order. At each, an NPC announces an act and a townsperson reacts. Each completed station adds a tax stamp to the Record.\]

| Station | Act | NPC line | Reaction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Docks | Sugar Act, 1764 | **CUSTOMS OFFICER:** Every barrel of molasses is checked now. Smugglers go to the admiralty court. No jury. | **MERCHANT:** No jury? Then a British judge decides everything. |
| Market | Currency Act, 1764 | **CLERK:** Colonial paper money is no longer allowed for debts. | **FARMER:** Then how am I supposed to pay anyone? |
| Print shop | Stamp Act, 1765 | **STAMP DISTRIBUTOR:** Every newspaper, legal paper, almanac, and deck of playing cards needs a tax stamp. | **KIT:** Paper? They're taxing *our* paper? This is the first tax Parliament has put directly on us. |
| Tavern | Quartering Act, 1765 | **SERGEANT:** The colony will provide quarters and supplies for His Majesty's soldiers. | **TAVERN KEEPER:** We pay for an army we never asked for. |

**KIT:** We didn't vote for Parliament. Not one colonist sits in it. That's the whole problem. No taxation without representation.

&#91;Choice moment — the colonists' response. Sivart chooses which protest to join first; all three are historically real and all get logged.\]

1. Join the boycott: refuse to buy British goods.
2. Attend the Stamp Act Congress, where nine colonies petition the King.
3. Watch the Sons of Liberty force the stamp distributor to resign.

&#91;Card: 1766. Parliament repeals the Stamp Act, the same day it passes the Declaratory Act, claiming the right to make laws for the colonies "in all cases whatsoever." 1767: the Townshend Acts tax glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea.\]

**SIVART:** So they took one tax away and then said they could tax anything they want.

**KIT:** Now you understand Boston.

**Metaphor Match (recap):** \[Seven picture cards show acts and events as metaphors, e.g., a parent taking a teenager's allowance to pay the family's bills. Sivart names each one, then builds one metaphor of his own from word tiles. Completing all seven grants XP and the Historidex card for the Sons of Liberty.\]

&#91;Archive Pages unlocked: AP-01 Grenville's speech, AP-02 imports chart, AP-05 Quartering Act, AP-06 Stamp Act, AP-08 tea boycott. Side quest markers: Daughters of Liberty, Committees of Correspondence.\]

## Script: Beats 4–7

### Beat 4 — King Street (Boston, March 5, 1770)

&#91;Night, snow on the ground. Redcoats have been stationed in Boston for over a year. A crowd gathers outside the Custom House. The scene plays as a short cutscene from a distance; no gameplay during the shooting.\]

**KIT:** \[running up\] Something's happened on King Street. Soldiers fired into the crowd. People are dead.

&#91;Card: Five colonists were killed, including Crispus Attucks, a sailor of African and Native descent. Captain Thomas Preston and eight soldiers were arrested.\]

**MR. SENOJ:** \[voice from the Record\] Every printer in Boston is about to tell this story. Before you believe any of them, weigh the evidence.

**Weigh the Evidence trial.** \[Courtroom set. The claim on the wall: "The soldiers fired on purpose at a peaceful crowd." Sivart examines seven exhibits and rates each from −2 (strongly disproves) to +2 (strongly proves). The scale tips as he goes.\]

| Exhibit | Archive Page | What it should teach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Revere's engraving, 1770 | AP-04 | Powerful propaganda, but made to persuade, not to record |
| Samuel Adams' writing on the "massacre" | AP-09 | A Patriot leader with a clear purpose |
| Captain Preston's deposition | AP-10 | The accused officer's own account; self-interest |
| Witness depositions from both sides | AP-11 | Eyewitnesses disagree; look for where they agree |
| John Adams' defense of the soldiers | AP-12 | A Patriot lawyer defending British soldiers |
| Chappel's etching, 1868 | AP-13 | Made nearly 100 years later; how distance changes a source |
| A video-game clip of the event | AP-14 | Fiction can feel real but carries almost no weight |

&#91;After rating all seven, the scale shows the player's verdict. Then the real outcome card: John Adams defended the soldiers in court. Preston and six soldiers were acquitted; two were convicted of manslaughter.\]

**SIVART:** Wait, John Adams? The Patriot? He defended the soldiers?

**MR. SENOJ:** He believed everyone deserves a fair trial. He said facts are stubborn things, and they can't be changed by our wishes. Keep that one.

&#91;Archive Page: AP-12 unlocks fully. Historidex: John Adams, Crispus Attucks.\]

### Beat 5 — Tea in the Harbor (Griffin's Wharf, December 16, 1773)

&#91;Card: 1770, Parliament repeals the Townshend duties except the tax on tea. 1773, the Tea Act lets the East India Company sell tea directly to the colonies, cheaper than smuggled tea, but the tax stays.\]

**KIT:** It's cheaper tea. That's the trick. If we buy it, we're agreeing Parliament can tax us.

**SIVART:** So it's not about the price. It's about the principle.

**KIT:** Now you're thinking like a Bostonian.

&#91;Night at the wharf. Members of the Sons of Liberty, some disguised as Mohawk Indians, board three ships. Minigame: haul tea chests to the rail and dump them, 342 chests, against the clock, while keeping the rule the protesters set: nothing else on the ships gets damaged.\]

**Record check — Sivart chooses:** What were the colonists protesting at the Boston Tea Party?

1. The Stamp Act
2. The Intolerable Acts
3. The Tea Act and Parliament's right to tax them ✔

### Beat 6 — The Squeeze (Boston and Philadelphia, 1774–1775)

&#91;Boston map darkens: warships block the harbor, soldiers in the streets. Shops are shuttered.\]

**KIT:** They closed the whole harbor. No ships in, no ships out, until we pay for the tea. And town meetings are basically banned.

&#91;The Intolerable Acts display as four broken links in the Record: the port closed, Massachusetts' government restricted, British officials tried outside the colony, troops quartered in empty buildings. A fifth link, the Quebec Act, extends Quebec into the Ohio Valley.\]

&#91;Travel to Philadelphia. Carpenters' Hall, September 1774.\]

**DELEGATE:** Twelve colonies are here. We will stop trading with Britain, and we will send a petition to the King. If he does not listen, we meet again in May.

**SIVART:** They're still asking the King nicely?

**MR. SENOJ:** Most of them still wanted to stay British. Watch how fast that changes.

&#91;Card: March 1775, Richmond, Virginia. Patrick Henry urges Virginia to arm its militia, ending with the famous words "Give me liberty, or give me death!" (as later recorded by his biographer).\]

### Beat 7 — One If by Land (April 18–19, 1775)

&#91;Night, Boston. General Gage is sending about 700 soldiers to destroy the colonists' weapons and supplies stored at Concord.\]

**KIT:** The redcoats are moving tonight. Mr. Revere needs the signal hung in the Old North Church: one lantern if they march by land, two if they cross the river by boat.

&#91;Stealth section: Sivart carries the lantern through Boston's North End, avoiding redcoat patrols by timing, alleys, and hiding spots. If spotted, he's sent back to the last checkpoint. No fighting.\]

&#91;Steeple: two lanterns are lit. They're crossing by water.\]

&#91;Revere rows across the Charles River past a British warship. Riding section across the countryside, waking houses: "The regulars are coming out!" Revere is captured near Lincoln; Sivart escapes on foot to Lexington.\]

**SIVART:** \[arriving\] It's almost dawn… they're already here.

&#91;Lexington Green, sunrise. Captain John Parker's militia stand in two lines. British troops march in. An officer orders them to disperse. A single shot rings out — the screen flashes white.\]

**MR. SENOJ:** Stop. Before we go on: who fired that shot?

**SIVART:** I didn't see.

**MR. SENOJ:** Neither did anyone else, apparently. That's a mystery for later.

&#91;History Mystery #2 marker appears on the green. Card: Eight militiamen were killed at Lexington. At Concord's North Bridge, the militia fought back and drove the British into a long, bloody retreat to Boston. The war had begun.\]

&#91;The Record's gray page now shows color. Mr. Senoj calls Sivart back to the Archive for the Chronicle Trial.\]

## Chronicle Trial

**Prompt:** How did tighter British control lead to armed conflict? Use evidence from at least three documents, and connect at least one long-term difference between colonial and British society (from Level 3) to the events of this level.

**Documents:** AP-01 through AP-08, your Revolutionary War DBQ set, plus any other Archive Pages the player has collected.

**How it plays**

1. **Claim.** The player writes a one-sentence claim, or builds one from word tiles ("British laws like \_\_\_ and \_\_\_ made colonists feel \_\_\_, which led to \_\_\_").
2. **Evidence.** The player drags three Archive Pages onto the evidence slots. Each slot asks for one sentence: what does this document show?
3. **Reasoning.** Sentence starters help connect evidence to claim: "This shows…", "This matters because…", "As a result…".
4. **Self-check.** Before submitting, the player ticks a checklist: my claim answers the question; I used three different documents; I explained *how* each one supports my claim; I included one long-term cause.
5. **Save.** The answer is stored in the Record (capped in length for the save code) and Mr. Senoj responds.

**Scoring guide (shown to the player after submitting)**

| Part | 3 — Strong | 2 — Getting there | 1 — Not yet |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Claim | Answers the question directly and names a cause and an effect | Answers the question but is vague | Restates the question or is off topic |
| Evidence | Three documents, accurately described, from more than one perspective | Two documents, or three with one inaccurate | One document or none |
| Reasoning | Explains how each piece of evidence supports the claim and links long-term and immediate causes | Explains some links | Lists evidence without explaining it |

The game can check the structure automatically (claim present, three documents used, starters filled in); the quality of ideas is self-scored with the guide, since nothing is graded in the game.

**Sample strong answer (Mr. Senoj shows it after submission)**

Claim: After the French and Indian War, Britain tightened control to pay its debts, and colonists who were used to governing themselves saw the new laws as attacks on their rights, which turned protest into war. Evidence: Grenville argued the colonies should help pay for their own defense (AP-01); the Stamp Act taxed colonists directly without any vote in Parliament (AP-06); colonial imports from Britain fell sharply during boycotts (AP-02). Reasoning: Because colonists had long elected their own assemblies, taxes passed by a distant Parliament felt like a loss of self-government. Each new law led to bigger protests, and each protest led to harsher British responses, until soldiers and militia met at Lexington.

## Side quests, History Mystery, and Ret Siger

### Side quest: Pontiac's War (frontier, 1763)

- **Setup:** At the trading post, the Lenape trader asks Sivart to carry a message to Fort Detroit, where the Odawa leader Pontiac and allied nations have the fort under siege.
- **Steps:** Hear Pontiac's council explain their anger: British officers cut off the gifts and trade that the French had kept up, and settlers keep crossing onto their land → then hear a British officer at the fort → log both views with the Perspective Lens.
- **Payoff:** Archive Page AP-15 (Pontiac's speech, as recorded in 1763); the Record links the war to the Proclamation Line.

### Side quest: Daughters of Liberty (Boston, 1768–1769)

- **Setup:** A group of women hosts a spinning bee to make homespun cloth instead of buying British cloth.
- **Steps:** Spinning rhythm minigame → deliver homespun to three households → a Loyalist shopkeeper complains about lost business.
- **Payoff:** Shows that boycotts needed women's work to succeed; ties to AP-02, the imports chart. Historidex: Daughters of Liberty.

### Side quest: Committees of Correspondence (1772–1774)

- **Setup:** Samuel Adams needs letters carried to towns around Massachusetts so they can coordinate resistance.
- **Steps:** A timed courier route on the map, picking the fastest roads → one town is Loyalist-leaning and won't open the door → deliver all letters before a British patrol intercepts the route.
- **Payoff:** Explains how colonies shared news and organized before the Continental Congress. Unlocks fast travel between Boston and Lexington.

### Side quest: The Loyalist Next Door (Boston, 1774)

- **Setup:** Mr. Abbott, a merchant across the street from Kit's shop, has "TORY" painted on his door.
- **Steps:** Talk to him: he is loyal to the King, depends on British trade, and fears the mobs that have tarred and feathered customs officials → Kit and Mr. Abbott argue; the player sides with neither and records both → the Fade grays out Mr. Abbott until the player logs his view fairly.
- **Payoff:** Perspective Lens practice; sets up Level 5's Patriot, Loyalist, or Neutral sorting.

### Side quest: Join, or Die *(fun)*

- **Setup:** Kit says Benjamin Franklin once joked that the colonies should ship rattlesnakes to Britain in return for the convicts Britain sent over. Franklin later drew a snake cut into pieces with the words "Join, or Die" (1754).
- **Steps:** Find eight snake segments hidden around the Level 4 maps; each is labeled with a colony or region.
- **Payoff:** The full cartoon for the Archive hub wall; Archive Page AP-16.

### History Mystery #2: Who Fired the First Shot?

- **Unlocks:** after Beat 7, at the marker on Lexington Green.
- **Evidence board:** eight exhibits from your Lexington CSI file:

| Exhibit | Source | Side | Date |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Massachusetts Provincial Congress, letter to the people of Great Britain | Colonial | April 26, 1775 |
| B | Deposition of 34 Lexington militiamen | Colonial | April 25, 1775 |
| C | A historical novel about Lexington (written for the game) | Fiction | Modern |
| D | Diary of Lt. John Barker, British officer | British | April 19, 1775 |
| E | The London Gazette report | British | June 10, 1775 |
| F | Diary of Ezra Stiles, retelling Major Pitcairn's account | Colonial (secondhand) | August 21, 1775 |
| G | An old school textbook account (written for the game) | Secondary | Mid-1900s |
| H | Personal narrative of Ensign Jeremy Lister, British officer | British | 1782 |

- **Steps:** Sort the exhibits by side, date, and type (primary, secondhand, fiction, textbook) → mark where accounts agree (the militia was on the green, they began to disperse, Pitcairn's horse was wounded) and where they differ (who fired) → rate reliability → write a CER answer.
- **Ending:** Any well-supported claim earns full credit. Mr. Senoj: "Historians still argue about this one. The skill isn't finding the answer, it's showing your evidence."
- **Payoff:** Historian Rank bonus; Historidex: Captain John Parker, Major John Pitcairn.

### Ret Siger: The King's Candy (Long Wharf)

- **Trace:** A bowl of candy at the docks labeled "Property of the King. Taxed." Nearby, a chalkboard reads "Pop quiz in 5 minutes."
- **Lesson:** Ret Siger assigns roles: King, two members of Parliament, two tax collectors, and a room full of colonists (Sivart and townspeople). Each colonist starts with ten candies. Parliament draws item cards (boots, hats, paper, buttons); anyone with that item pays.
- **Choices for each tax:** pay; pay out of fear of punishment; hand over an IOU; or smuggle (hide candy, with a chance of being caught). Each choice changes Sivart's candy count and the townspeople's mood.
- **The split:** Collectors keep 10%, Parliament 50%, the King 40%; Sivart watches the King's pile grow while colonists go empty.
- **Debrief:** Your "Classroom vs. Reality" chart: match each moment (the surprise announcement, no one consulted, paying out of fear, IOUs and smuggling) to the historical reality, then answer two of your debrief questions: What was unfair about how the class was taxed? How could it have been handled more fairly?
- **Student Achievement:** "Period 1 boycotts the candy tax so well Parliament repeals it."

## Debate Duel, Timeline Weave, and Study Mode

### Debate Duel: Patriot vs. Loyalist (Kit vs. Mr. Abbott)

The player picks a side, then the D20 rolls a quality the round is judged on (fair, costly, legal, dangerous, popular). Each round the opponent plays a claim and the player answers with the evidence card that best fits it and the rolled quality. Using a card from the other side's perspective to concede a point earns a bonus, rewarding fairness.

| Patriot cards | Evidence |
| --- | --- |
| No taxation without representation | Colonists had no members in Parliament (AP-06) |
| We already govern ourselves | Colonial assemblies had made their own laws for over a century |
| Our rights as Englishmen are being ignored | Admiralty courts tried smugglers without juries |
| Britain is punishing the innocent | The Boston Port Act closed the harbor for everyone |
| The colonies must unite | Committees of Correspondence and the First Continental Congress |

| Loyalist cards | Evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Britain protected us; we should help pay | Britain's war debt nearly doubled defending the colonies (AP-01) |
| Colonial taxes are low | Colonists paid far less tax than people in Britain |
| Mobs are more dangerous than taxes | Customs officials were tarred and feathered |
| Trade with Britain keeps us prosperous | British imports and markets supported colonial merchants (AP-02) |
| Rebellion against the King is treason | Rebels could lose their property or lives |

### Timeline Weave: torn page order

The player places these on the Record's timeline, then draws arrows from each cause to its effect. Correct links glow; at least five links are required to repair the page.

1. 1754 — Washington surrenders Fort Necessity; the French and Indian War begins
2. 1763 — Britain wins; Proclamation of 1763; Pontiac's War
3. 1764 — Sugar Act and Currency Act
4. 1765 — Stamp Act and Quartering Act; Stamp Act Congress and boycotts
5. 1766 — Stamp Act repealed; Declaratory Act
6. 1767 — Townshend Acts
7. 1770 — Boston Massacre
8. 1772 — Committees of Correspondence
9. 1773 — Tea Act; Boston Tea Party
10. 1774 — Intolerable Acts and Quebec Act; First Continental Congress
11. March 1775 — Patrick Henry's speech
12. April 1775 — Lexington and Concord

Key cause-and-effect links: war debt → new taxes; Stamp Act → boycotts → repeal; Townshend Acts → troops in Boston → Boston Massacre; Tea Act → Tea Party → Intolerable Acts → First Continental Congress; colonial militia preparing → march on Concord.
